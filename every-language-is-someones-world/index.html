    Self-study activities

    Every Language Is Someone's World

    Activities on first languages, dialects, plurilingualism and language policy, for readers working on their own.

    Based on: Kostoulas, A. (2026). Every Language Is Someone's World: Reflections on International Mother Language Day 2026. Keep the post open in another tab while you work.

    Who this is for

    Student teachers, practising teachers and anyone studying language education who wants to work through the post independently. You do not need a tutor or a group. The post grew out of a lecture for students in Greece, but the activities work for readers from anywhere.

    How it works

    • A warm-up, nine activities and a closing reflection, each on its own page. Use the contents list to move between them.
    • Activities 3 to 8 each follow one section of the post. Read the section, then do its activity before moving on.
    • Every activity ends with a Self-check. Sometimes it gives an answer. More often it tells you how to know you have done enough, because many tasks are about your own experience and have no single right answer.
    • The whole set takes about three and a half hours. Spread it over several sittings, and save your work each time.

    Nothing you type is saved or sent anywhere. Your answers live only in this browser tab and disappear when you close or reload it. To keep them, enter your name under "Your work" and save a Word document or a text file.

    If you are not from Greece Many of the post's examples are Greek. You do not need background knowledge to follow them.
    • Wherever an activity says "Greece" or "Greek", you can substitute your own country and its main language, or do both and compare.
    • When the post lists communities in Greece (Pomak, Romani, Arvanite, Vlach and others), you do not need to know them. The point is how long the list is in a country that presents itself as monolingual.
    • "Idioma" is explained in the post itself, and the Greek phrase in footnote 7 is translated there.
    • You have one advantage: you are living the post's distinctions right now. Look for boxes like this one, which carry an extra prompt for you.

    Before anything else

    Warm-up

    About 5 minutes

    Think of one word or expression from the way people spoke at home when you were small that you cannot translate well into any other language you know. Say it aloud.

    Self-check

    You are ready to go on when you have one word and one person in mind. Keep both; you will need them at the end.

    Before you read

    Activity 1. My languages

    About 15 minutes

    List every way of speaking you have ever used, including school languages, home varieties, regional speech, and languages you only understand. For each one, note where you learned it, with whom you use it, and how it feels.

    If you are stuck: think of a grandparent, a holiday, a song, a job, a prayer, a game.

    Self-check

    Does your list contain at least one item that is not a named national language? If not, look again at how you speak at home, with old friends, or online.

    You will return to this list in Activities 3 and 6.

    Before you read

    Activity 2. What do I think?

    About 5 minutes

    Mark each statement. Do not look anything up.

    Self-check

    You have marked all six, honestly, including the ones where you suspect the "expected" answer differs from yours. You will come back to them in Activity 9.

    While you read

    Activity 3. Whose first language?

    Read the section "What Even Is a 'Mother Language'?" first

    The post gives four criteria for a first language: heritage, early acquisition, proficiency and identity. Tick the criteria that each case meets. Use the third-generation Greek American from the post, and two varieties from your own list.

    Self-check

    Can you state, without looking, the two reasons the post gives for preferring "first language" to "mother language"? If only one comes to mind, reread the subsection on rethinking the term.

    While you read

    Activity 4. Who drew the line?

    Read the section "The Language/Dialect Problem" first

    For each case in the post, note three things: whether speakers can understand each other, whether we speak of one language or several, and what explanation the post gives for the label.

    Your own case

    If you are not from Greece Take your case from your home country.
    Self-check

    Your notes should show intelligibility and labels pulling in opposite directions in at least two cases. If they do, you have found the section's argument.

    You should also be able to explain the Weinreich line and the term "variety" in your own words.

    While you read

    Activity 5. Under the surface

    Read the section "The Myth of the Monolingual Society" first

    Choose one task
    If you are not from Greece The post names French and German among the national languages that were actively constructed. For the Place task, you could instead use the street where you live now and describe it as a newcomer sees it.
    Self-check

    Have you named at least one language or variety that official descriptions of the place or family would leave out?

    Can you say, using the post's words, why the monolingual society is called a "political construction"?

    While you read

    Activity 6. Getting the terms straight

    Read the section "The languages we speak" first

    Label each situation, using the post's definitions.

    From list to repertoire

    Return to your Activity 1 list and turn it into a repertoire. Add registers, professional vocabularies, and moments when you mix.

    If you are not from Greece Add yourself as situation (f).
    Self-check

    Use "Check my answers" above for situations (a) to (e). For (f), apply the same test as (a) and (c): is this the dominant language of the community you are living in?

    Your repertoire is complete enough when it includes at least one thing that is not a "language" at all, such as a register or a habit of switching.

    While you read

    Activity 7. Three lenses

    Read the section "Language Policy: Who Decides?" first

    Choose a setting you know from the inside, such as a school, a workplace or your family. Describe it through the post's three dimensions.

    If you are not from Greece Your exchange programme is a good setting: which languages are assumed, which are required, and which do students really use with each other?
    Self-check

    You have one sentence per dimension and at least one point where they conflict.

    If your ideology and management sentences say the same thing, remember that ideology is belief and management is formal decision.

    While you read

    Activity 8. Deficit or resource?

    Read the section "The World We're Actually Living In" first

    Rewrite each statement so that it treats the child's language as a resource.

    "He has no language yet."

    "She can't join in until her Greek improves."

    "They should speak only Greek at home."

    Self-check

    The last paragraph of this section of the post answers the third statement directly; your rewrite should agree with it.

    For the other two, check that your new sentence names something the child can do.

    After you read

    Activity 9. Looking back

    About 20 minutes

    Here are the six statements from Activity 2, with what you said then. For each, write: "I used to think… Now I think… because the post says…" It is fine to remain unconvinced; say what would change your mind.

    A language is a world

    0 words

    Self-check

    Compare your six answers with the Summary and the questions-and-answers section at the bottom of the post.

    Each of your "because" clauses should point to something the post says, not only to your own opinion.

    To finish

    Closing reflection

    About 15 minutes

    The word

    Go back to the word from the warm-up, and reread the last two lines of the post.

    The change

    The next step

    The post ends by asking us to take seriously the languages of the people around us. Name one person, one place and one concrete thing you will do differently, as a teacher, a student or a neighbour.

    Self-check

    Your next step is concrete enough if someone else could tell, a month from now, whether you did it.

    To finish

    Beyond the trails

    Find and read something for pleasure in, or about, one of the less dominant varieties in your repertoire: a story, song lyrics, a recipe, a community page.

    Tips for reading alone: start with the shortest text on your trail. If an academic article feels heavy, read the abstract, introduction and conclusion, then decide. Do not look up every unknown word.

    If you are not from Greece Some of the linked posts deal with Greece in detail. If one loses you, switch trails, or read it alongside something about the same topic in your own country and log both.

    Reading log

    After each text, write three lines only.

    Self-check

    There is no target number of texts. Ask yourself only two things: did I read at least one text to the end because I wanted to, and does my log contain a question I did not have before?
